The strongest idea in your material is **“Worth keeping.”** Your work is about finding value in what already exists: a Glasgow housing block, a church hall, the knowledge passed between you and your father while building a cabin.

That gives the website a distinctive point of view. The ambition should be an architectural story you can move through, with 3D revealing how you think and photographs showing what that thinking becomes.

**The idea and first impression**

The site should feel like an inhabited architectural drawing: precise lines, generous space, tactile materials, and occasional transitions from drawing into building.

A proposed opening:

> **Marta Kowalczyk**  
> **Make an old building worth keeping.**  
> Architect working with existing buildings, housing and timber. Glasgow.

This borrows directly from the position you express in your biography. It establishes what you care about before introducing your employment history.

The central visual is a shallow, three-dimensional section of Calder Court, reconstructed from approved drawings. As the visitor scrolls, the existing building stays in place while the insulation and winter-garden intervention come into view. The motion explains your architecture: retaining, adding, improving.

Use a small, persistent navigation: **Work / Approach / About / Contact**, with your name returning to the top. A visible “Explore the work” link lets people jump straight to the projects.

My working assumption is that the audience includes prospective collaborators, practices, clients, and teaching contacts. The site should introduce you personally while making the authorship of practice projects explicit.

**What sits behind the homepage**

Each project gets its own shareable page. The homepage introduces the argument; these pages provide enough evidence to understand the work.

Every project page should include:

- A short account of the existing condition or design question.
- Location, dates, status, scale, practice, and your specific role.
- The key decisions, explained through selected photographs and drawings.
- Outcomes, with qualifications and sources for quantitative claims.
- Full collaborator and photography credits, followed by the next project.

The composition should vary with the material. Calder Court needs before/after evidence and the wall detail. The cabin deserves a construction diary. Kirk Street should centre on the section. The school needs selected, legible extracts from its six boards, with full boards optionally available to inspect.

Drawings should open into a zoomable viewer with captions and scale information. Visitors should be able to read the architecture without downloading a huge PDF.

Photography should supply most of the colour. Avoid artificial sepia treatments or simulated paper wear.

Use **Instrument Serif** for a few large statements and **Manrope** for body text, navigation, and captions. The combination brings warmth to the personal writing while keeping technical material clear. Both can be self-hosted.

The layout should alternate expansive images with tightly composed text and drawing spreads. Fine rules, numbered figure captions, and generous margins provide an architectural rhythm. Keep paragraphs comfortably narrow and body text around 18px on desktop. No tiny labels that require the visitor to lean into the screen.

**How the motion earns its place**

The Calder section is the signature 3D moment. It should derive from the real building information, with retained and added elements clearly distinguished. There should be no invented geometry presented as project documentation.

Keep its scroll-linked sequence within roughly one additional screen of travel. After that, release into the project story. Preserve native scrolling throughout.

Elsewhere, motion has three jobs:

- **Explain a change:** align a drawing with a photograph or reveal an intervention.
- **Show a sequence:** introduce stages of the cabin’s construction.
- **Guide attention:** gently bring in a caption or selected detail.

Avoid perpetual rotation, cursor effects, background particles, and motion on every image. The photographs and drawings need periods of stillness.

If the available drawings cannot support a convincing 3D section, use a carefully layered sectional illustration. The concept remains intact, and the site can still feel exceptional.

**How it holds up on a phone**

Mobile gets a deliberately composed version of the same story:

- Start with the opening statement and a strong static view of the section. Load optional 3D only where device capability allows.
- Replace the pinned section sequence with three short, labelled frames: existing, intervention, result.
- Stack before and after photographs, with their labels always visible.
- Show a tightly edited cabin sequence; keep video tap-to-play.
- Crop competition boards into readable details, with access to the full drawings.
- Use single-column text, comfortable margins, and touch targets of at least 44px.

No sideways scrolling is required to understand a project. No information depends on hovering.

Reduced-motion preferences should produce an equally complete static experience. Keyboard navigation, visible focus, descriptive image text, diagram explanations, and sufficient contrast belong in the initial design.
