**The opening and the journey**

Use the drawing-set order as the homepage’s sequence, with plain-language titles and small secondary labels such as “03 / Sections.” Keep Work, About and Contact available throughout, so visitors can bypass the narrative.

1. **Location — start with a place and a person.**

   Open with a strong photograph of the cabin in its surroundings, selected once the images are available. Pair it with:

   > **A place, carefully worked through.**  
   > I’m Marta Kowalczyk, an architect in Glasgow. My work centres on existing buildings, housing and building with timber.

   Below: “Associate at Field & Rowe Architects · ARB registered · Studio tutor.”

   A short introduction connects your father’s workshop to your current practice. This gives the website a personal origin immediately, without turning the opening into a lengthy biography.

   The photograph stays still. As the visitor scrolls, its context gives way to a cabin plan.

2. **Plans — show how you think about space.**

   Introduce the cabin as your complete authorship: design, drawings, photographs and construction, built with your father in 2021–2022.

   Place a hand drawing alongside the corresponding CAD plan. Use two or three annotations to explain actual decisions, chosen after reviewing the drawings: how the cabin sits on the site, how its 28 square metres work, or how an opening relates to its surroundings.

   This is where a person with a small plot should begin to recognise the value of your thinking. Let them understand a spatial choice before inviting them into a full case study.

3. **Sections — give the site its signature 3D moment.**

   Develop a simplified, accurate model of the cabin from your drawings. Over one short desktop scroll sequence, move from its overall form to a cutaway and then its timber frame. Stop at a viewpoint that corresponds to a real construction photograph.

   Labels explain only what the drawings and photographs establish. No speculative joints or invented assembly sequence.

   Follow with the frame-raising time-lapse, played on request. This connects the model to something you actually built.

   The result should make a small building understandable to a prospective client. It also makes the site memorable through material that you own.

4. **Elevations — introduce the work of keeping buildings.**

   Change scale to Calder Court: 96 flats, completed in 2025. Subject to permissions, show the existing and completed conditions, followed by a concise account of the retrofit.

   Put your role beside the project title, where visitors will see it:

   > **Calder Court — Field & Rowe Architects**  
   > Project architect from technical design to handover, responsible for detailing, the wall build-up and site delivery. Concept design by a practice partner.

   Present the performance claim as:

   > **68% lower modelled heating demand.**  
   > In-use performance has not yet been measured. Monitoring is planned to begin in winter 2026–27.

   Confirm the calendar wording before publication and update this statement as monitoring progresses.

   Kirk Street can follow as a shorter example of conversion: a church hall becoming six flats. Its inclusion needs an accurate account of your contribution and permission for the selected material.

5. **Details — make your expertise tangible.**

   Pair two details, if practice permission permits: a cabin timber joint and the Calder Court wall build-up you drew. Give each a drawing, a built photograph where available and a short explanation of the problem it solves.

   This is the strongest place to connect workshop knowledge with professional delivery. On desktop, a drawing can stay visible briefly while its explanation passes alongside it; avoid another elaborate animation.

   Mention the Swiss timber-joint book as a formative influence in your own words. A photograph of your working copy could be considered after checking what is appropriate to reproduce; do not depend on scans of its pages.

   If the Calder detail cannot be published, this section works entirely through cabin material.

6. **Schedules — show the breadth and discipline behind the work.**

   Borrow the clarity of a schedule for a compact project index: project, type, status, your role and practice or collaborators. Use stacked entries on phones.

   Give *A School That Grows* a selected section or structural drawing, with this attribution:

   > Shortlisted competition proposal, 2024. Four-person team; my contribution was the structure and sections.

   Name the team and practice affiliation once confirmed. Present the project as evidence of structural and spatial thinking, with the six full boards optional and subject to permission.

   Move naturally into your Year 2 teaching at Clyde School of Architecture, including a direct invitation for guest-critic enquiries.

7. **The next conversation — finish with a person, not a project grid.**

   Close with a short biography, a working photograph if available, CV and two clear contact routes:

   > **Have an old house or a small plot in mind?**  
   > Get in touch to start a conversation.

   > **Looking for a guest critic?**  
   > Contact me about studio reviews and teaching.

   Use email links initially. Explain your current practice position clearly; the wording should not imply that an independent practice has already launched.

**Where the RIBA stages and snag list belong**

The drawing set structures the homepage. RIBA stages structure the deeper project information.

Each case study should identify **where you were involved**, rather than suggesting you performed every stage. For Calder Court, technical design through handover provisionally maps to Stages 4–6; Stage 7 monitoring remains a clearly distinguished future activity unless your involvement is confirmed.

A short “How a project develops” page can translate Stages 0–7 into the questions a homeowner has: what is possible, what permissions are needed, how it gets built and how it performs afterwards. Describe the process without prematurely advertising a full independent service.

The snag list informs the final case-study chapter: **“Checking the finished work.”** Use an approved, anonymised example only if it helps explain your care on site. Avoid turning unresolved defects or confidential site records into decoration.

**Colour, type and motion**

Keep warm chalk `#F3F0E8`, charcoal `#252923` and pale stone `#DEDCD3`. Use deep oxide `#854333` sparingly for annotations and links. Let the cabin’s real timber tones bring warmth through photography.

Use Source Serif 4 for large, personal statements and Inter for body text, project facts and drawing labels. Drawing numbers, fine rules and aligned captions supply structure. Avoid distressed paper effects or ornamental drafting marks.

The cabin receives the sole major 3D sequence. Elsewhere, movement reveals a relationship between drawing and building. Use native scrolling, with no forced pauses or introductory loading screen.

On phones, replace the scroll-controlled model with three labelled stills: whole cabin, section and frame. Stack drawings and photographs, allow drawings to enlarge, and keep every explanation visible without interaction. Reduced-motion visitors get the same static sequence.
