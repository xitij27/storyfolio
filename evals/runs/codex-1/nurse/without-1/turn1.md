I’ll review the material first, then shape a site concept around your work and personality, including the scroll experience, visual direction, mobile behavior, and practical requirements. I won’t build anything.


The site should be built around your question: **“Why does the system make the safe thing the slow thing?”**

That is more distinctive than a portfolio about “innovation in healthcare.” Your material shows someone who understands the pressure of a shift, notices where systems fail nurses, and makes practical changes. The website should let people experience that way of thinking.

My proposed concept is **“Making the safe thing easier.”** A dimensional, scroll-led story that starts with the complexity of a ward and gradually brings clarity to it. The visual centerpiece is a handover board: information arranged in layers, then reorganized as you explain your work. The 3D has a purpose—it makes the work of improving a system visible.

The primary audience would be nursing leaders, clinical informatics hiring teams, and potential research collaborators. They should leave knowing what you noticed, what you changed, and what evidence supports it.

**The journey, from top to bottom**

1. **An opening that sounds like you**

   A large, editorial headline:

   > Making the safe thing easier.  
   > Eleven years in nursing. A practical eye for the systems around it.

   Your name and role sit above it. I would use **“Aoife Brennan — Clinical Nurse Manager 1 · Nursing Informatics Link Nurse.”** Your notes distinguish the formal, one-day-a-week informatics role from the “Clinical Informatics Lead” wording on LinkedIn. The site can show leadership through the work without overstating the appointment.

   Beside the text, a shallow 3D composition of handover sheets, board lines, and annotation tabs slowly settles into place. These would be illustrative, using clearly fictional content.

   The first screen includes direct links to **Selected work** and **Contact**, with a compact navigation for Work, Approach, and About. Visitors can skip the visual sequence.

2. **The shift: why this work matters**

   As the visitor scrolls, the board turns gently toward them. Overlapping information becomes a readable arrangement.

   The anchor is your note: **“The night we had nine admissions.”** A short account would explain what that night demanded of the team and why the whiteboard captures the job. The material gives us the premise; your own account would supply the detail.

   I would commission a clean, fictional reconstruction inspired by the board, rather than publish the ward photograph. It can retain the density and urgency without exposing clinical information.

   This is one brief visual transition, not a long animation visitors must sit through. It ends with your central question about safety and speed.

3. **The main case study: why nurses skip the scan**

   This should receive the most space because it already has a strong evidence trail.

   Begin with **71% → 94% medication scanning over nine months**, followed immediately by the investigation: **40 observation hours and interviews with 22 nurses.**

   Three illustrated observations appear as the reader scrolls:

   - Wristbands that stopped scanning after a shower.
   - Scanners stored away from the medication room.
   - Printed barcode sheets carried in pockets.

   Each observation resolves into the corresponding change: different wristband stock, scanners fixed beside the medication room door, and stopping the printed sheets. Small spatial movements show the relationship between a problem and its fix.

   Your line **“None was laziness”** deserves prominent treatment. It reveals your approach more clearly than a list of skills.

   The result stays visible as a simple, labeled comparison. We have two reported percentages, so I would not invent a month-by-month chart. Explain that this is a **23 percentage-point improvement in scanning**, without claiming a measured reduction in medication errors or proving causation.

   Credit the 2025 conference poster and provide its abstract in accessible text. A fuller case study should clarify your role, collaborators, and how scanning was measured.

4. **The second case study: a better handover**

   The dimensional board flattens into a document spread: the old sheet and the redesigned sheet.

   On desktop, they appear side by side, with a few annotations explaining the actual changes once you supply the sheets. As the reader moves down, corresponding areas highlight together. Avoid making a drag-to-reveal slider the only way to compare them.

   The established outcome is **“Now used on six wards.”** We should not claim time saved or fewer omissions unless you have evidence.

   This section shows that your work extends beyond diagnosing a problem to designing something colleagues adopt.

5. **The person behind the improvements**

   The motion becomes quieter. A warm portrait, if you want one, sits beside a short first-person introduction about remaining close to clinical practice while studying informatics.

   Supporting details provide breadth:

   - Preceptor to 14 newly qualified nurses.
   - Falls and pressure-ulcer audit dashboards.
   - Experience from coronary care to managing shifts on a 28-bed cardiac step-down ward.

   Present dashboards as supporting work until there are suitable examples and enough context for a full case study. Tie Power BI, basic SQL, ISBAR, and audit skills to their uses rather than displaying proficiency bars.

   A short career timeline can include the MSc, with its current completion status confirmed before publication.

6. **An invitation with a clear purpose**

   End with the now orderly board, mostly still, and a simple line:

   > Better systems start by listening to the people using them.

   Follow with a specific invitation to discuss clinical informatics, medication safety, or nursing workflow improvement. Provide email, LinkedIn, and an accessible CV download. No contact form is necessary for the initial site.

**The visual direction**

Use the visual language of working documents, with the care and space of an editorial publication. Avoid literal hospital branding or a futuristic medical interface.

| Element | Proposed treatment |
|---|---|
| Background | Warm paper, `#F4F1E9` |
| Main text | Deep ink, `#172C32` |
| Primary accent | Dark teal, `#246B68` |
| Occasional emphasis | Muted rust, `#A44732` |
| Rules and surfaces | Pale gray-green, `#DCE4DE` |
| Headlines | **Newsreader**, bringing warmth and an editorial voice |
| Body and navigation | **Source Sans 3**, clear and practical |
| Figures | Large, aligned numerals with plain-language labels |

Color would never be the only way to distinguish a finding or outcome. Final combinations need contrast testing.

The 3D should feel tactile: paper thickness, slight shadows, restrained perspective. One recurring object connects the story. No floating medical symbols, spinning heart, custom cursor, or constant background movement.

**On a phone, the story becomes simpler**

The same content remains in the same order, but the board becomes a lightweight static composition or a short, gentle transition. Nothing essential depends on rendering 3D.

Case studies stack into readable sequences: observation, change, result. Handover sheets appear one after the other with matching annotations and an option to inspect each at a larger size. There is no sideways scrolling or prolonged pinned section.

Use native scrolling everywhere. Respect reduced-motion preferences with a fully still presentation, and keep all text, navigation, and evidence available without animation. Keyboard access, visible focus, readable type, and generous touch targets belong in the initial design.

**What else the site needs**

The initial scope should be a homepage, two individually linkable case studies, an accessible CV, and contact links. A blog can wait until you have a reason to maintain one.

Before design is finalized, gather:

- Your account of the nine-admissions shift.
- The old and new handover sheets, recreated with fictional data and cleared for public use.
- Confirmation of your individual contribution and collaborators on each project.
- Any available context for the scanning figures, plus the poster file.
- Confirmed role wording, MSc status, preferred contact details, and an optional portrait.

The patient-specific medication near-miss from LinkedIn should be generalized if used; the bed number and patient identifier add nothing to the public story. Hospital identification, internal documents, and colleague imagery also need appropriate publication clearance.

Technically, the site needs fast text-first loading, optional deferred 3D, responsive images, search and social-sharing metadata, and accessible HTML for every case study. It should remain useful if the visual effects fail to load.

The defining moment is **watching a difficult working situation become easier to understand—and then seeing the evidence of what you changed.** That gives the motion a reason to exist and makes the portfolio unmistakably yours.
