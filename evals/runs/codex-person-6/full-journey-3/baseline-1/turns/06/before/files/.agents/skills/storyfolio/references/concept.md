# The concept

The second stop: from the fact sheet to one world of ready pieces, shown running, which the person accepts or changes.

## What the site is

An introduction of the person, in this order. A chapter is left out only when the person, asked at the first stop, has nothing for it.

| # | Chapter | Holds |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hello | Their name, what they do in one line, what they are looking for |
| 2 | Where from | The places they have lived and moved between |
| 3 | What they studied | Their education, and what in it shaped them |
| 4 | Where they have worked | Their roles over the years: where, what, when, what they owned |
| 5 | What they are good at | Their field, and how deep they go in it |
| 6 | What they have made | Every project in brief, one or more in depth |
| 7 | What others say | Their words, one at a time, if there are any |
| 8 | Get in touch | How to reach them, and a CV |

Everything on the scroll is brief. More opens on a click: a project's window or page, the whole CV.

## The pieces

Three worlds can each tell every chapter, drawn only from the person's data: a sheet of paper that bends into each chapter's picture, a map of their places, and a pop-up book. Tell the whole site in one world: each chapter then grows out of the last by that world's own move. Put a chapter in another world only when the person asks; the dive that joins it reads as a picture popping in. All three run in the gallery, with two made-up people: https://xitij27.github.io/storyfolio/transitions/

Use the pieces as they are, with only the person's data in them: leave `stitch/` and the page around the story unchanged, and put nothing over the scene. Write a scene only when the person asks for one.

## Choosing the world

Choose the world that shows the most of this person with the captions hidden, given what their facts hold: how many places, jobs and projects, and whether anyone is quoted. Write why, and a line for each chapter on what it shows of them, from the fact sheet.

## Showing it

For a requested build, scaffold the site and fill it as `engine/README.md` says under "The person's site": their confirmed facts, and nothing else, in `content/person.ts`, and the world in `content/mix.ts` (such as `WHOLE.map`). Answer the Person, Backdrop, Honesty and Thirty seconds tests below for it. Check it at desktop and phone size and look at every picture. Nobody can picture a site from words, so give the person its address to scroll. For a planning-only request, give the world in words with the gallery's address.

Put to them, in this order:

1. the world, and why;
2. what of their facts each chapter shows, and what it leaves out;
3. the gallery's address, and that they can choose another world there, ask for changes, suggest a new component, or describe a design of their own.

Write the world, the reasons and their answer to `docs/story/concepts.md`.

## A scene of your own

When the person asks for a new component or describes a design of their own, build it as a world, as `engine/README.md` says, and hold it to all that follows. Write in `docs/story/concepts.md` what it shows in each chapter it holds, how it joins its neighbours, and the lines of the fact sheet it stands on.

## What does not work

- One project told in depth in place of the person.
- One set of pieces rearranged through every chapter: bars, tiles or sockets that line up, split and light. Or a chart in place of a scene.
- 3D as a backdrop behind the text.
- One 3D scene, however good.
- A different effect for every chapter with nothing joining them. Together they are a tour of effects.

## The through-line

In a scene of your own, one world ties the chapters together, and it looks like the person's field to a colleague at a glance: the systems someone runs, the instruments someone builds with, a cook's kitchen. Paper is for people whose work is paper. Within that world, something that can change shape becomes each chapter's picture.

The site this skill came from belongs to a researcher, and its object is a paper's nine pages. The front pages of the papers they learned from hang in the air and fly in to pile up as the paper. The pages bend into the gores of a paper globe for where they have lived. They lie as a desk where scattered data points gather into clusters, for how they work. A maze is printed across them as a floor and solved as the reader scrolls. One photograph spans all nine, grey on one side of a seam and colour on the other. They hang as a wall of plates, a plate for each project, each opening its window.

Sheets bend, tile and fold into a globe; light, lines, particles and nodes can draw a route, a building or a machine. Rigid bars and blocks can only be rearranged. It comes from what the person works with and makes, never the trade's emblem: a chef's hat, a server rack, a stethoscope. For anyone who works with data, the emblem is a field of points that gathers into a chart. It fits every analyst alive, which is what is wrong with it.

The chapters may take names from the world, as a paper's sections, while holding the chapters above in their order. A question the close answers can tie the site together too. Leave it out rather than force one, and check each premise it rests on against the fact sheet: an employer's field is not the person's contribution. A plain introduction is the fallback, not the aim.

## What each chapter's scene shows

A picture of that chapter, which a visitor would name with the captions hidden: a globe or a route for where from, the place they studied, a line of the places they worked, the things of their field, the work itself. A scene that shows only that something moved is not done.

Make each from what the person told you: their answers to the picture questions, or one particular thing in their material. A scene that explains something is a diagram: choose and draw it by `references/diagrams.md`. A label turned into its symbol, a degree into a circuit board, a job into its logo, could be anyone's.

For each chapter, write at least three candidate pictures before choosing one, at least one from something only this person said. Drop every candidate that is a rearrangement.

The scenes are one world the camera travels through. Each grows out of the last as the reader scrolls; nothing appears or vanishes at a chapter's edge.

At least one project is shown working, in miniature, worked by the scroll or a control: the maze is solved, the photograph takes its colour.

Where the person has pictures of their own work, the scenes show them. Where they have none, the scenes picture the kind of thing they work with, labelled as illustration.

**The opening shows where the person comes from:** what they learned from, or what they began with. The scene may gather, be put together, unfold, be drawn, develop or switch on. At the close the world is whole again.

## Test each one before you show it

Write your answer to each test that applies: for the pieces, the four that "Showing it" names; for a scene of your own, all of them. Rework what fails.

| Test | Ask |
|---|---|
| Person | Would a visitor know who this is, where they come from and what they do, and not only one project? |
| Backdrop | With the captions hidden, does each scene show what its chapter is about? |
| Working | Does at least one project run? |
| Swap | Could it sit unchanged on a colleague's site? Then it is not theirs yet. |
| Field | Would a colleague know their field from the look of the world? |
| Emblem | Is a scene the symbol of a job or a label, rather than this person's work and what they told you? |
| Seams | Does each scene grow out of the last, with nothing popping in? |
| Honesty | Does any sentence in it say more than the fact sheet does: a ranking, a reason, which came first, how a thing was left, a day, a place? |
| Thirty seconds | Can someone who does not scroll get the name, the work, some proof, and how to reach them? |
