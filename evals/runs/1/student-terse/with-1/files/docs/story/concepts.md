# Concepts — Mina Park

Reader: the person who reads placement applications at an engineering firm. Two minutes. [facts.md]

## A. Keep, fix, or replace

**The question.** Keep, fix, or replace?

**The form.** Her own ritual, already in the material: the inventory she laid out on the garage floor and labelled keep / fix / replace, which is a parts-inspection sheet — a real document of the trade (alongside the job card and the set of drawings). Chapters, in a parts sheet's order:

| Chapter | Role |
|---|---|
| Laid out on the floor | Opening — name, the question, the garage floor itself |
| Part: Mina Park | Who — Hallam, second-year, what she's after |
| How she inspects | How — job card discipline, logbook, lathe/mill, SolidWorks/MATLAB/Python |
| Part: the pedal box bracket | What — 18% lighter in CAD and simulation; not yet made; machines in November |
| Part: the hand-crank charger | What — gear train and housing, hers; it worked, badly: 4 minutes of cranking for 1% of charge |
| Part: the cracked hub flange | What — found hiding under the grease on the restoration; the wheel was never true because of it |
| Verdict | What came of it — three honest verdicts: fix, fix, replace — nothing finished, nothing overclaimed |
| Take her on | Close — the reader is asked for the same call she makes on a part: keep, fix (give her a placement and watch what she does with it), or pass. Contact. |

**The object.** The parts themselves, laid out at rest like the garage floor photo: the pedal box bracket, a gear and a housing shell from the charger, a hub flange with its crack, a wheel segment, a bottom bracket cup. Five to seven real, specific shapes — not a generic gear or wrench. They sit in a grid at rest, the way the blog photographs them, and group into one exhibit per chapter.

**What it becomes.** Opening: the parts drift into the floor grid as the page loads. "Part: the bracket" and "Part: the charger": each lifts out of the grid alone, turns to show the CAD surface or the housing, and a line of real numbers appears next to it. Close: the parts settle back into the grid, and a one laid out where Mina's own card would go, verdict blank, waiting on the reader.

**What it stands on.** Every fact line in facts.md's Projects and Jobs sections; the "keep, fix, replace" ritual is already hers, from the blog post list.

**What it needs from her.** The garage-floor photos (she has these). The bracket's CAD/sim images. Whether the charger has any photo at all, or whether its piece on screen is built plain, from its real dimensions, with no photo texture — ask her. Her yes that "take her on" can frame a placement decision as a parts verdict; some people would find that cute and some would find it a bit much.

**Why it is theirs.** The ritual is in her own blog post list before any of this was planned; nobody invented it for her. A colleague's site with a different inventory would not have a cracked hub flange or a bracket waiting on a machine.

## B. The job card

**The question.** How do you know it works?

**The form.** The job card, which she named directly as what she works from: what the customer says is wrong, what she finds, the parts, what she did, the test ride.

| Chapter | Role |
|---|---|
| A card goes on the board | Opening — name, the question |
| Customer says | Who — who she is, what she's asking for (a placement) |
| What I find | Where from / How — Hallam, the co-op, the workshop tools |
| Parts | What — the three projects, each as a part on the card |
| What I did | What came of it — the work on each, honestly: one simulated, one tested and found wanting, one in progress |
| Test ride | Close — nothing here has finished its test ride yet; the bracket's is in November. Contact. |

**The object.** A stack of workshop job tags, five to eight, each a card-shaped tile that can carry a photo or a CAD render on its face. At rest they sit clipped to a board, the way tags hang at the co-op.

**What it becomes.** Cards fan out across "Parts," each face turning to show one project. At "Test ride" they stack back up, one left face-out: blank, dated for whenever the reader replies.

**What it stands on.** The job-card description she gave verbatim; the three projects.

**What it needs from her.** The same images as concept A. A yes that the "test ride" close, read plainly, doesn't sound like a sales line.

**Why it is theirs.** It's the exact ritual she named, in her own words, not a generic "process" page.

## C. The logbook

**The question.** What does it take to be sure?

**The form.** The workshop logbook, her other named ritual: an entry at a time, each with what she found and what came next. The blog's five posts already run this way — a flat tyre, an inventory, stripped paint, a wheel that was never true, a bottom bracket.

| Chapter | Role |
|---|---|
| First entry | Opening — "Thirty pounds and a flat tyre," name, the question |
| Entry: who's writing | Who |
| Entry: the workshop | How — tools, the co-op, the discipline of logging a job |
| Entry: the bracket / the charger / the hub | What — one entry per project, dated |
| Entry: result | What came of it |
| Next entry: blank | Close — undated, an invitation to write the next one together. Contact. |

**The object.** Pages of a spiral logbook, six to nine, that turn on a spine. Each page carries one entry's content on its face; at rest they sit closed as a single bound book.

**What it becomes.** Pages lift and turn through the entries; at the close, a blank ruled page faces the reader.

**What it stands on.** The logbook, named directly; the blog's own post order.

**What it needs from her.** Same images. Confirmation that "an entry at a time" doesn't read as slower or smaller than it should.

**Why it is theirs.** The blog already reads as a logbook before any of this; nothing is added to make it one.

## Picked

**A, "Keep, fix, or replace."** Chosen by Mina, following my recommendation.

## Which I'd choose

**A, "Keep, fix, or replace."** It and C both come straight from material she wrote before any of this was planned, which is what makes either honest rather than styled on. A wins because its object is a line-up of her actual parts rather than pages or cards, so the 3D is never standing in for a document — it's standing in for the parts themselves, which is closer to what she makes. It's also the most direct answer to a two-minute reader: three parts, three plain verdicts, then one blank row with her name on it. B reads a half-step more like a sales pitch ("test ride" as a close); C's "undated, an invitation to write together" risks sounding soft for an audience with two minutes. A states its uncertainty (bracket untested, charger weak, hub cracked) as plainly as its results, which matches what she asked for directly: don't make this look like more than a second-year.
