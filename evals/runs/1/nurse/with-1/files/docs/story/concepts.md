# Concepts

The question behind all three, taken from her own words on LinkedIn ("the person who asks why the system makes the safe thing the slow thing"), turned forward: **What does it take to make the safe thing the fast thing?**

---

## Concept 1 — The handover (ISBAR cards)

**The question.** As above.

**The form** — ISBAR, the handover she rewrote and that now runs on six wards: Identify, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation.

| Chapter (ISBAR order) | Role | Holds |
|---|---|---|
| Open | The opening | A stack of blank handover cards, waiting |
| Identify | Who | Her name, CNM1 / Nursing Informatics Link Nurse, 11 years, 6 in charge |
| Situation | Where from | BSc 2011–15, Coronary Care 2015–19, Step-Down 2019–present, MSc 2024–26 |
| Background | How | ISBAR, the early warning chart, the care plan, the audit cycle — and, softened to nothing identifying, the night that started it |
| Assessment | What | The poster: 40 hours of observation, 22 interviews, three workarounds found |
| Recommendation | What came of it | 71% to 94% in nine months; the handover sheet on six wards; 14 nurses precepted |
| Close | The close | The question answered; how to reach her |

**The object.** Eight to ten blank ISBAR cards, index-card size. At rest: a tidy fanned stack on a handover desk. As Who: they fan open like a hand, her name across their faces. As Background: they flip to show the five ISBAR letters as a diagram. As Recommendation: they arrange into a bar climbing from 71 to 94. At the close: back to a stack, a contact tab on top.

**What it needs.** Nothing beyond the fact sheet. One thing to check with her: whether a handover-card motif, even shown empty, sits too close to the ward for comfort, given how carefully she's drawn the line around patient material.

**Why it's hers.** It's the exact document she personally rewrote and spread to six wards — not an illustration of "a nurse's handover," but the one she wrote.

## Concept 2 — The audit cycle (the PDSA wheel)

**The question.** As above.

**The form** — Plan, Do, Study, Act: the cycle she names as one of the things she works from every day, and the exact shape her own poster study already took.

| Chapter | Role | Holds |
|---|---|---|
| Open | The opening | A wheel, blank, one quarter-turn from closing — the night that started it, told with nothing identifying |
| Who | Who | Her name, titles, 11 years, 6 in charge |
| Plan | Where from / How | The years, the places, the tools she reads a ward by: ISBAR, the chart, the care plan |
| Do | What | The 40 hours of observation, the 22 interviews, the three workarounds found — none of them laziness |
| Study | What came of it | The scanners moved, the wristbands changed, the sheets stopped; 71% to 94% in nine months |
| Act | What came of it | The handover redesign now on six wards; 14 nurses precepted; dashboards built for two more audits |
| Close | The close | The wheel turns full circle and stops on a new Plan: the question answered, and how to reach her |

**The object.** A circular dial in four wedges, Plan / Do / Study / Act, five to eight smaller panels nested in the wedges carrying the real figures (71, 94, 40, 22, 6, 14). At rest: the wheel sits whole, like the cycle poster on a ward noticeboard. Through the page it turns one quarter-chapter at a time. At the close it completes its turn and settles, facing a new, empty quarter — her next plan.

**What it needs.** Nothing beyond the fact sheet.

**Why it's hers.** PDSA is the one structure in the material that is both a tool she names using every day and the literal shape of her best-documented piece of work — it isn't borrowed for the site, it's where the poster's own four sections came from.

## Concept 3 — The care plan (the tabbed chart)

**The question.** As above.

**The form** — the nursing care plan: Assessment, Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation, Evaluation (ADPIE), the document written at every bedside to take a problem from noticed to resolved.

| Chapter | Role | Holds |
|---|---|---|
| Open | The opening | A closed chart, tabs showing at its edge — the night that started it, told with nothing identifying |
| Assessment | Who / Where from | Her name, titles, years, places |
| Diagnosis | How | The problem as she found it: 29% of doses given unscanned, and why — three workarounds, none of them laziness |
| Planning | What | The fixes planned: scanners moved, wristbands changed, printed sheets stopped; the handover rewritten with four others |
| Implementation | What came of it | Rolled out: six wards carrying the new handover, 14 nurses precepted |
| Evaluation | What came of it | 71% to 94% in nine months; dashboards watching two more audits since |
| Close | The close | The chart's last tab turns blank — the next problem to take on is her own next job; the question answered, and how to reach her |

**The object.** Five to seven coloured tab dividers, the kind that mark a problem in a bedside chart, fanned at rest like a closed folder's edge. As Diagnosis: they spread to show one workaround each. As Planning/Implementation: they line up in the order the fixes happened. As Evaluation: they turn green, in sequence, as the figure climbs past 94.

**What it needs.** Nothing beyond the fact sheet.

**Why it's hers.** It's the document that runs under everything a ward nurse manages — not a metaphor fetched from outside nursing, but the one she fills in for every patient, turned here to the ward itself as the patient.

---

## Which I would choose

**Concept 2, the audit cycle.** Two things decide it. First, it needs nothing invented: every chapter already has real figures and real actions behind it, because the poster was written in this shape before the site asked for one. Second, it speaks the one language her stated audience already shares — PDSA is not a nursing-only form, it's the quality-improvement language of hospitals and the companies that sell them systems, which is exactly who she said this site is for. The handover (Concept 1) is more intimate and closer to the ward, but it risks sitting a little close to the patient-chart material she's drawn hard lines around, even shown empty. The care plan (Concept 3) is well-grounded but its "ward as patient" framing at the close is a reach the fact sheet doesn't quite hand me — it would need a sentence that isn't fully hers yet.
