# Concepts

Three, for Rafael Costa. He named his own daily tools unprompted: "the analysis memo (the question, the data, what I did, what I found, what I would do), the dashboard, and the weekly report." All three concepts are built from one of these three, so none of them is a guess at his trade — each is something he already said he works from.

---

## Concept 1 — The Memo

**The question.** What makes a dashboard something people actually open?

**The form** — the analysis memo, in the five parts Rafael himself named:

| Chapter | Role |
|---|---|
| The question | Opening |
| Who's asking | Who |
| The data | Where from |
| What I did | How |
| What I found | What, and some of what came of it |
| What I'd do | The close |

(Six chapters — "What others say" folds into "What I found," as a quoted line, rather than its own chapter; there's one quote on file, not enough to carry a chapter alone.)

**The object** — seven memo cards, one per real piece of work: the Atlantico churn analysis, the Helix sample tracker, the Helix weekly scheduling report, the Lisbon Rent Map, the FraudShield capstone, the Health Data Hackathon charts, AutoPilot. Simple flat rectangles, like index cards with a clipped corner. At rest they sit as one neat stack, the way a finished memo sits on a desk.

**What it becomes.**
- *The data:* the seven cards lay themselves out along a line, oldest to newest — Porto to Lisbon, DataTugas to Meridian Bank.
- *What I did:* one card — the Helix scheduling report — unfolds into its own five faces, showing the memo's anatomy worked through on a real example.
- *What I found:* all seven cards fan out into a wall, each showing its real face: the Rent Map's own screenshot, a chart from the FraudShield notebook, the hackathon's chart, a plain recreation of the Helix panel layout, and a text-only card for AutoPilot and Meridian, where nothing can be shown.

**The opening and the close.** It opens on a single blank card with just a question mark where "the question" goes — the part of the memo he always starts with. It closes on one finished card again, filled in with the site's own question, answered, and "What I'd do" written as the invitation to get in touch.

**What it stands on.** The memo structure he named; every project in the fact sheet; the Beatriz Lima quote; the Meridian confidentiality limits.

**What it needs from him.** A Rent Map screenshot. A FraudShield chart or notebook screenshot. His yes on a stylised, no-real-data recreation of the Helix panel layout (not an actual screenshot). A photo of himself, if he wants one on the opening or closing card.

**Why it's theirs.** No one else's site can open on a blank question mark and close on "what I'd do," because that order of thought — question, data, what I did, what I found, what I'd do — is the one he said, unprompted, is how he actually works.

## Concept 2 — The Weekly Report

**The question.** What does a week's work look like, once it's over?

**The form** — the weekly report, the other ritual he named:

| Chapter | Role |
|---|---|
| This week | Opening |
| Prepared by | Who |
| Last week | Where from |
| Method | How |
| This week's numbers | What |
| Change vs. last week | What came of it |
| Notes from the team | What others say |
| Next week | The close |

**The object** — eight analog gauges, dial-and-needle instruments built from a cylinder and a thin needle, one per real, confirmed number: Helix report time (five hours to two), Rent Map visitors (3,000 a month), AutoPilot's users (six), the hackathon team (five), DataTugas (twelve people), and three more marking years, not metrics, where a number isn't the point. At rest, they hang together like a wall of meters in a plant room.

**What it becomes.**
- *This week:* every needle rests at zero — the report hasn't been written yet.
- *This week's numbers:* each needle sweeps, one at a time, to its real reading as its project is introduced.
- *Change vs. last week:* the gauges that moved (report time, visitor count) pair up with a plain before/after, nothing claimed beyond what's confirmed.

**The opening and the close.** It opens on a stack of blank report pages, Monday's ritual about to start. It closes on the final page, signed, with "Next week" rewritten as an invitation to work together.

**What it stands on.** The same fact sheet as Concept 1, read through its confirmed numbers rather than its projects.

**What it needs from him.** The same images as Concept 1; nothing additional, since this concept leans on numbers over pictures.

**Why it's theirs.** The before/after shape — five hours to two, 3,000 a visitor floor instead of the flattering 5,000 — is the actual shape of his best outcomes, not a generic "metrics" section; the gauges only move because he gave real readings to move them to.

## Concept 3 — The Ticket

**The question.** How does a request turn into something people actually use?

**The form** — not one he named directly, but the shape every one of his projects already took: someone asked a question, he scoped it, built it, and it got used. This is also the language of the product-analyst job he's chasing, in a product team's own words:

| Chapter | Role |
|---|---|
| New ticket | Opening |
| Reporter | Who |
| Backlog | Where from |
| In progress | How |
| Shipped | What |
| In use | What came of it |
| Comments | What others say |
| Next sprint | The close |

**The object** — seven ticket plates, flat rounded rectangles like a kanban card, one per real ask: the Atlantico churn question, the Helix sample-tracking ask, the Helix scheduling-report ask, the Rent Map (an ask he gave himself), FraudShield (his capstone brief), the hackathon brief, AutoPilot. At rest they sit stacked in one column, unsorted, like a fresh backlog.

**What it becomes.**
- *Backlog:* the seven plates spread into a single column, oldest at the bottom.
- *In progress → Shipped:* each plate physically slides across the board, backlog to in progress to shipped, as the chapter is scrolled — the one piece of motion in the whole page that is also literally what a product analyst's work does.
- *In use:* the shipped plates pick up a real, confirmed line each — "cut the weekly report from five hours to two," "3,000 visits a month," "six people use it every week."

**The opening and the close.** It opens on one new, empty ticket with just a question mark in the title field. It closes with a "next sprint" ticket already titled with the job he's after, assigned to whoever's reading, with his contact as the only field left to fill in.

**What it stands on.** The same fact sheet; it reads every project as an ask rather than a possession, which keeps it honest about the hackathon (one ask, five people) and about AutoPilot (an internal ask at Meridian, not a public product).

**What it needs from him.** The same images as Concept 1, plus his comfort with the "ticket" framing reading as a little knowing about the product-company job search — it's honest, but it is also clearly aimed at that audience, more than the other two.

**Why it's theirs.** A kanban board is a common shape, but this one only has his seven real asks on it, each ending where it actually ended — a notebook that never ran anywhere, a script six people use, a map 3,000 people a month actually visit. Nobody else's backlog reads the same way.

---

## Which I'd choose

**Concept 1, The Memo.** It's the one most directly in his own words — he didn't just mention the memo, he gave its five parts unprompted, in that order, before I asked. That makes the opening (a blank question mark) and the close ("what I'd do," turned into an invitation) earn their place rather than being chosen for effect. Concept 3 is the sharpest pitch to the product-company audience he's targeting, and is a legitimate second choice if he wants the site to lean harder into that pivot — but a kanban board is a shape a reader has seen before, even if the cards on it are entirely his; the memo's shape is not.
