## Someone with thirty seconds

The first screen (1a) carries his name and "I make dashboards people actually use." A rail, visible from the first screen on, jumps straight to any chapter. A "Get in touch" link, also visible from the first screen, skips straight to Chapter 6 — so the fourth thing a thirty-second visitor needs, how to reach him, is one click away without reading anything else. The one thing the first screen does not carry on its own is a proof figure; the rail's label for Chapter 5 ("What I found") doubles as that promise, since the wall is where the real numbers sit.

## The look and the type

Three looks, each a reading of "a memo" as a material. Rafael scrolls through a built version of each before choosing; this is what each would feel like.

**A — Office memo.** Ground: warm off-white, like real paper (`#F6F3EC`). Ink: near-black (`#1C1B19`). One accent: a stamped clay red (`#B5452B`), used only for the question mark, the live figures in Chapter 5, and links. Card surface: matte, faint paper texture, a true drop shadow — it should look liftable.

**B — Dashboard dark.** Ground: near-black slate (`#14181C`). Ink: off-white (`#EDEFEF`). One accent: a cool teal (`#3FD6C3`), used only on numbers and the active rail dot. Card surface: dark glass, a thin glowing edge — closer to the BI tools he actually uses all day.

**C — Clean report.** Ground: plain white. Ink: graphite (`#2B2E33`). One accent: a single banking blue (`#1E3A5F`), used only for links and the Chapter 6 call to action. Card surface: flat, a hairline border, no shadow — the most minimal of the three, and the one with the least room for error if the 3D is ever thin.

My own lean is **A**, because "memo" as a material means paper, and a stamped accent colour reads as a mark made by hand on a real document, not a UI choice. **B** is the stronger pick if he'd rather the site read like the tools he builds, not the paper he writes on.

Two typefaces: a humanist sans for all the surrounding prose (the captions, the nav, the long-form text) — something built for reading at length, not display. A monospace, typewriter-styled face for anything printed on a card's own face — the memo's five labels, headers, figures — since that belongs to the form, not to the page around it.
