# Concepts

Three ways to tell Rafael's story, each built from a different form of his own trade. All three answer some version of the line he gave us himself: "I make dashboards people actually use."

---

## Concept A — The memo

**The question.** What has to be true before someone trusts a number enough to act on it?

**The form** — the analysis memo, the first thing he named as what he works from:
1. Question *(opening — his name, the question who's asking)*
2. Data *(where from — the places and years, as evidence)*
3. What I did *(how — his way of working)*
4. What I found *(what, and what came of it — the work shown working, then the outcomes, Beatriz's words folded in as evidence)*
5. What I would do *(the close — his answer, and how to reach him)*

**The object** — a deck of eight index cards, recognisable at rest as a small stacked deck. Each carries one real thing on its face: a job, a number, the hackathon team, the Beatriz quote, Lisbon Rent Map's visitor count.

**What it becomes.**
- *Question:* the deck opens from a single card bearing the question, written as if just typed.
- *Data:* cards spread and line up left to right into a timeline — Porto, Porto, Lisbon, by year.
- *What I found:* cards turn face-up as exhibits — one becomes a line from 5 hours to 2, one becomes Beatriz's sentence, one becomes the rent map's 3,000.
- *Close:* cards square back into a single stacked deck, the top card now his contact details.

**What it stands on:** every confirmed job, number, and quote in the fact sheet.

**What it needs from him:** nothing further to build; a real screenshot later would only replace a card, not change the shape.

**Why it's his:** the memo's five-part shape is the exact ritual he said he works from — not a layout borrowed for the occasion.

## Concept B — The dashboard

**The question.** What makes a dashboard something people actually open?

**The form** — a dashboard's own anatomy, the second thing he named:
1. Title bar *(opening — his name, the question, loading in)*
2. Date range *(where from — jobs and years as a time filter)*
3. Filters *(how — his tools: SQL, dbt, Tableau, Power BI, as filter chips)*
4. Charts *(what — the work shown working: the Helix hours line, the rent map, AutoPilot's weekly run)*
5. KPI tiles *(what came of it — 200+ staff, 3,000 visitors, 60% faster, 6 users, a team of 5)*
6. Annotation *(what others say — Beatriz's line, as a note pinned to the chart it's about)*
7. Export *(the close — one tile left, his contact details, like a share link)*

**The object** — nine rectangular tiles, sitting at rest as a dashboard grid, grey and empty until the story gives them something to show.

**What it becomes.**
- *Title bar:* tiles sit as loading skeletons, then resolve into his name as the question types in above them.
- *Charts:* tiles expand into the real shapes — one draws the 5-to-2-hour line, one outlines Lisbon by parish, one ticks off six users running AutoPilot.
- *KPI tiles:* tiles flip to oversized numbers, one per confirmed figure.
- *Export:* the grid collapses into a single tile — his name, role, and how to reach him — the dashboard, closed.

**What it stands on:** the same confirmed facts as A, arranged by what a dashboard actually contains rather than what a memo contains.

**What it needs from him:** nothing further; it's built entirely from numbers already confirmed.

**Why it's his:** a dashboard isn't a decoration here — it's the actual thing he builds for a living, so the object and the subject are the same thing.

---

## Concept C — The weekly report

**The question.** What turns a week of work into something someone else can act on by Monday?

**The form** — the weekly report, the third thing he named, and the one AutoPilot exists to fill in:
1. Period *(opening — "Week of…", his name, the question as the header)*
2. This week's work *(where from / what — jobs and projects, one per sheet, shown working)*
3. Headline numbers *(what came of it — the same confirmed figures, framed as this week's results)*
4. Flagged *(what others say — Beatriz's line, styled as a flagged item, good news instead of a risk)*
5. Next steps *(the close — his answer, and how to reach him)*

**The object** — a desk pad of eight tear-off sheets, sitting at rest as a stacked pad with one week showing on top.

**What it becomes.**
- *Period:* the top sheet of the pad carries the question as its header, dated.
- *This week's work:* sheets peel off one at a time and lay out left to right — Atlantico, DataTugas, Helix, Meridian, Lisbon Rent Map — a timeline made of torn-off weeks.
- *Headline numbers:* each sheet's small print enlarges into the real figure it was hiding — 2 hours, 3,000, 6, 5.
- *Next steps:* the sheets restack into the pad, the visible sheet now showing his contact details.

**What it stands on:** the same confirmed facts, read through the rhythm of a recurring report rather than a one-off document.

**What it needs from him:** nothing further to build.

**Why it's his:** the weekly report isn't borrowed — AutoPilot, one of his own real projects, exists specifically to fill one in every week. The object is the exact artefact his work produces on a schedule.

---

## Which I'd choose

**Concept B, the dashboard.** Rafael's own line — "I make dashboards people actually use" — already names the dashboard as the thing that connects every job and project he has. Making the object a dashboard means the medium and the message are the same thing on screen, which gives the thirty-second read ("oh, this person builds dashboards, and this page is one") without a word of explanation. It's also the most visually alive of the three for a scroll-driven 3D build: tiles loading, expanding into real charts, and flipping to numbers gives the "things move as you scroll" feeling asked for, built entirely from data he's confirmed rather than invented screens.

A and C are both honest and workable — C in particular has a nice detail (AutoPilot literally produces the form the object is built from) — but B is the tightest fit to how he described himself and to what the site needs to do for him: get a product analyst job by proving, in the page itself, that he can make a dashboard someone wants to open.

**Decided: Concept B, the dashboard.** Rafael confirmed this.
