## A — The job card (chosen)

- **Question:** “How do you know a part is ready to work?”
- **Form:** Mina's five confirmed job-card fields, in their order: Reported fault (opening and who); Findings (where from and how); Parts (selected work and all three projects); Work carried out (her contribution); Test ride (outcomes and close). The headings are literal: bracket and charger evidence never becomes a claim of a bike test ride. The last chapter distinguishes bike test rides from bench observations and untested CAD.
- **Object:** Six paper job-card leaves in a shallow stack. Two faces per project across six leaves: bike inventory and repair-writing evidence; bracket CAD and its status record; charger gear-train/housing evidence and reported prototype result. These are newly designed portfolio records, explicitly not copies of co-op customer job cards. Simple rectangular planes, paper thickness and small corner radii. Faces hold actual work evidence or confirmed text; no fabricated customer entries, signatures, dates or checkmarks.
- **Three arrangements:** Findings separates two bike leaves into a comparison of the reported symptom and the published hub finding. Parts opens all six into three paired project records. Test ride arranges outcome leaves into visibly different states: bike test ride as part of her work process, charger worked poorly, bracket not physically tested.
- **Opening:** An inventory image, if cleared, fills the first leaf: the bike parts on the garage floor, taken from her supplied blog description and confirmed photo availability. The six leaves gather into the proposed portfolio job-card stack. Name, student description, three work links and contact action are immediately visible. This is a visual treatment of her documented inventory, not an invented origin story.
- **Close:** The leaves restack with one aligned edge. Answer: “A result needs a check. Here is what has been checked—and what hasn't.” Contact action: discuss a mechanical or product-design placement. Do not call the bracket ready.
- **What it stands on:** `facts.md`: project records; blog entry 2 and entry 4; Mina's Q2, Q4 and Q7 answers; her two-minute audience and request for honest scope.
- **What it needs:** Cleared inventory and repair images, cleared CAD, charger imagery if available, public contact. The written-evidence fallback remains meaningful if images are withheld.
- **Why it is hers:** It uses the exact job-card sequence Mina works from, pairing the cracked hub finding, the inefficient charger and the unmachined bracket without smoothing away their different outcomes.

## B — The workshop logbook

- **Question:** “What can a working prototype tell you that a model can't?”
- **Form:** Six editorial logbook headings: Entry (opening/who); Context (where from); Observation (how); Design (what); Check (outcomes); Next entry (close). These are proposed headings, not a claim about her actual logbook layout.
- **Object:** Eight thin logbook leaves in a closed, landscape notebook, carrying confirmed project descriptions and conditional work images. No imitation handwriting, made-up dates or invented annotations.
- **Three arrangements:** Context stands leaves in a short chronology of confirmed work dates. Design opens them into project spreads showing bike, bracket and charger. Check places the bracket model opposite the charger's reported physical result, with their different evidence labels.
- **Opening:** Notebook opens onto the confirmed distinction between Mina's co-op job card and workshop logbook; her identity and work links remain immediately readable. No fabricated formative workshop incident.
- **Close:** Leaves close around an unfilled “Next entry” space and a placement contact action. Answer: a model and a prototype provide different evidence; her bracket is still awaiting manufacture while her charger has a reported physical result. Do not invent a specific lesson she says she learned.
- **What it stands on:** `facts.md`: Q2, Q4 and Q7; CV skills with workshop-induction qualification; confirmed project attribution.
- **What it needs:** Any cleared logbook excerpt, or explicit permission to use a designed portfolio notebook rather than a reproduction; cleared project imagery and public contact. No genuine logbook excerpt is currently supplied.
- **Why it is hers:** The page revolves around her precise current contrast: an 18% CAD reduction awaiting machining and a working charger that performed poorly.

## C — The parts inventory

- **Question:** “What is worth keeping, fixing or replacing?”
- **Form:** Six inventory sections: Inventory (opening/who); Identification (where from); Condition (how); Keep; Fix; Replace (work, outcomes and close). The first three are editorial expansions; keep/fix/replace comes directly from her bike post. Actual classifications of individual bike parts must come from cleared evidence, not guesswork.
- **Object:** Eight rectangular inventory tags on a tabletop-like plane, each with an image of a real part or confirmed project record. The tags are an editorial rendering of her labelled inventory, not evidence she used physical tags. Recognisable as a grouped parts inventory at rest.
- **Three arrangements:** Inventory places tags beside a cleared overhead photograph. Condition brings the documented cracked-hub finding into focus. Fix brings bracket redesign and charger work into paired exhibits, with status captions rather than claims that either solved an invented problem.
- **Opening:** Parts laid out on the garage floor, using the inventory image if cleared. Name and direct project/contact links sit beside the inventory immediately.
- **Close:** Tags return to an ordered inventory. Answer: decisions need evidence of condition; show known findings and known limits, then invite a placement conversation. No invented list of parts actually kept or replaced.
- **What it stands on:** `facts.md`: blog entries 2 and 4; restoration; Q2/Q4 and photo availability.
- **What it needs:** Cleared inventory photographs and actual part classifications. Without those, this concept needs substantial text-only substitution and loses specificity.
- **Why it is hers:** It begins with her own garage-floor inventory and its keep/fix/replace labels, rather than a generic engineering emblem.

## Selection and tests

**Choose A, the job card.** Mina authorized judgment on uncovered matters and said to carry on. This selection requires no further taste decision. It uses her explicitly described daily process, fits five compact chapters, and lets a recruiter distinguish contribution from outcome quickly. B is strong on comparison but risks implying a supplied logbook that we do not have. C relies more heavily on image rights and actual part classifications, and gives less natural space to the other two projects.

- Swap: each contains Mina's specific work; A additionally uses her confirmed field order.
- Backdrop: object faces carry evidence; arrangements communicate symptom/finding, project pairing and validation state. Equivalent content and relationships must remain in the accessible HTML fallback.
- Depth: A runs from a stated problem through evidence and contribution to a check; B runs from model to observation; C from inventory to decisions.
- Emblem: no decorative car, gear, bike model or generic engineering symbol. The objects are the records used to make work inspectable.
- Honesty: no competition achievement, repair count, testimonial, verified strength, invented client or unconfirmed lesson.
- Thirty seconds: all concepts expose identity, three work links, a proof preview and contact action at entry. A is shortest for the stated two-minute audience.

Selection is a proposed design direction chosen under Mina's authorization, not a factual claim about her preferred visual taste. Storyboard approval remains pending.
