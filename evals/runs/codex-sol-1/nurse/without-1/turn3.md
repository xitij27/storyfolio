The direction is **“From ward practice to better clinical systems.”** The site should help hospital and clinical system hiring teams see three things clearly: you understand nursing work, you can find the reasons a workflow fails, and you can help colleagues make practical improvements.

Its opening statement is **“Making the safe thing easier.”** The visual identity comes from the documents you work with every day. The strongest evidence comes from the scanning study and ISBAR redesign.

This is the complete proposed plan. Nothing will be built at this stage.

**The site structure**

One main page tells the story in order, supported by two dedicated case-study pages:

- Medication scanning: observations, workarounds and ward improvements.
- ISBAR handover: leading a five-person redesign group.

A compact navigation contains **Work, Approach, About, Contact**, plus a **CV** link when the document is ready. Your name links back to the top. Visitors can jump directly to information without following the entire scroll sequence.

**2. Approach: begin with how the work actually happens**

This section explains your perspective before presenting results.

Suggested opening:

> A clinical workflow has to work during a busy shift. I start by watching what people do, understanding why they do it, and finding changes that make safe practice easier.

A short sequence describes your approach:

**Observe → Understand → Work with colleagues → Change → Review**

The supporting paragraph connects this to the audit cycle—plan, do, study, act—without claiming that every project used a formally documented PDSA process.

**The 3D:** The opening paper settles into a flat surface. A fine line travels between the stages, highlighting each as its explanation enters view. By the end, the visual behaves like an ordinary diagram.

**Visitors can click:** Links to the scanning and handover projects. The diagram itself does not need interactive controls.

**On a phone:** The stages appear vertically with their explanations alongside them.

**Without 3D:** A static sequence communicates the same approach. There is no hidden text revealed only by scrolling.

**3. Featured project: understanding missed medication scans**

This is the main evidence section.

Opening title:

> **Understanding the workarounds behind missed scans**

Introduce the setting as an observational study on one cardiac ward. Explain that 29% of doses were being given without a scan, then present the work in three stages.

| Stage | What the section shows |
|---|---|
| Observation | Forty hours across day and night shifts, with short interviews involving 22 nurses |
| Findings | Wristbands failing after showers; scanners located away from the medication room; printed barcode sheets carried in pockets |
| Changes | Scanners positioned beside the medication room door, wristband stock changed, printed sheets stopped |

The text should retain the important observation from your abstract: these workarounds saved nurses time; they were not evidence of laziness.

**The 3D:** A simple spatial diagram shows the relationship between the medication room and a scanner positioned away from it. It is labelled as an illustration, not a hospital floor plan.

As the visitor scrolls, the diagram highlights the three obstacles. Each is paired with readable text. When the changes are discussed, the scanner moves beside the medication room door and the other illustrated obstacles resolve. No simulated patient, medication administration or operational barcode appears.

On desktop, this visual can remain alongside the text for a short stretch. Scrolling stays native and visitors can move through at their own speed.

**Attribution is prominent:**

> **My contribution**  
> Forty hours of observation, identifying the three workarounds, and getting the scanners moved to the medication room door.
>
> **A ward team improvement**  
> Pharmacy, IT, night staff and I contributed to the changes.

End with a clear, static result:

**Ward scanning: 71% → 94% over nine months**  
**An increase of 23 percentage points**

Keep “ward” and the timeframe next to the figures. Do not use the result as a personal achievement claim or imply that scanning alone proves a reduction in patient harm.

Credit the Irish Nursing Informatics Conference 2025 poster.

**Visitors can click:** Read the full case study; download the poster when a suitable file is available.

**On a phone:** Each obstacle and change appears as a compact illustration directly beside its explanation. There is no pinned scene.

**Without 3D:** Static diagrams show the original conditions and changes. The findings, attribution and result remain identical.

**4. Featured project: leading the ISBAR handover redesign**

Opening title:

> **A shared redesign, adopted on six wards**

Supporting line:

> I led a group of five to redesign the ISBAR nursing handover form.

Show the empty old and new forms. Add a small number of annotations explaining meaningful changes after the actual sheets have been reviewed.

Expand ISBAR once in a caption: **identify, situation, background, assessment, recommendation**. The section should concentrate on the redesign rather than explaining a framework familiar to its audience.

The story needs four elements:

- The problem the group was trying to address.
- Your role in leading the five-person group.
- The reasoning behind the changes.
- Adoption on six wards.

Do not claim time savings, fewer errors or improved safety unless supporting evidence becomes available.

**The 3D:** Two paper sheets initially sit at slightly different angles, then settle side by side as the comparison begins. After that, document reading takes priority: no rotation or movement while a visitor examines a form.

**Visitors can click:** Before / After controls, enlarge each empty form, and Read the full case study. Enlarged views include accessible close controls and keyboard support.

**On a phone:** One form appears at a time with large Before / After buttons. Annotations sit below the relevant image, rather than relying on tiny hotspots.

**Without 3D:** The forms appear immediately in the same comparison layout.

**The look**

The overall feel is an editorial portfolio with the physical texture of working documents.

| Element | Direction |
|---|---|
| Background | Warm ivory `#F4F1E9` |
| Main text | Deep ink `#182A2D` |
| Links and emphasis | Deep teal `#176B68` |
| Selected annotations | Muted terracotta `#B85F48` |
| Supporting surfaces | Pale sage `#DCE5DC` |
| Large statements | Source Serif 4 |
| Body, navigation and evidence | Source Sans 3 |

Use large headings, generous margins, fine rules and precise captions. Paper surfaces have subtle shadows and texture. Body text remains ordinary selectable text, never baked into the 3D scene.

Colour should support the hierarchy without suggesting clinical alert levels. Links and chart distinctions also need labels or shapes, so colour is never the only cue.

**Motion and fallback behaviour across the site**

The 3D is an enhancement to a complete page. Every section has its text, evidence, links and static visuals available independently.

- Native scrolling throughout; no forced pauses or scroll-controlled navigation.
- No essential hover interaction.
- Reduced-motion settings receive still compositions.
- Phones use the lighter layouts described above.
- If 3D fails or cannot run well, the static version remains usable.
- Heavy assets load after the opening content and only when needed.
- Animation pauses when it is off-screen.
- No sound, flashing effects or animated number counters.

Keyboard navigation, visible focus states, readable contrast and meaningful image descriptions are required. Document images need accompanying text that explains their relevant content.
