# The storyboard

The third stop: the script of the whole page, which the person says yes to. Write it to `docs/story/storyboard.md`.

A plan covers what its asker thought to ask for. Most people ask for the idea and for what moves. Nobody asks what the site does on a phone, or without the 3D, or what a shared link shows, and a script that leaves those out is half a script. So this one has a place for each, and an empty place shows.

## The chapters

A row to a chapter:

| Chapter | Its name | What it holds, from the concept's list | What it tells, in a few words | What its scene pictures | How it grows out of the last | What you can touch |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

For a site made from the pieces, a chapter's scene is its piece, and it grows out of the last by its world's move: say what of the fact sheet it shows.

## The beats

Under each chapter, a row to a beat. A beat is one settled state of the scene and one block of text.

A full story normally has twelve to twenty-two meaningful beats. Add up their screen heights, state the total, and trim repetition. Do not pad or invent facts to reach the range. If the confirmed material calls for a shorter story, propose that exception explicitly for the person's approval.

From the pieces, a chapter is one beat of 1.9 screens, and the range does not apply.

| Beat | Screens | What the scene shows | The pictures and words in it | What its caption stands on |
|---|---|---|---|---|

- **Screens:** how much scroll the beat takes. One, or more where the scroll works something.
- **What the scene shows:** what a visitor would name with the caption hidden: the person, a piece of their work, the place or the things of their field. "The pieces move", "a gap opens" or "one lights up" is not an answer. Say also what of the last scene turns into this one as the reader scrolls.
- **The pictures and words in it:** the person's own pictures, each named, and any illustration of the kind of thing they work with, labelled as one. Words in the scene are readable at the size shown, or left to the caption. A category name, such as Method or Results, shows nothing.

Every project is listed in brief on the scroll, and each opens its detail on a click: a window or its own page.
- **What its caption stands on:** a working title of a few words, and the lines of the fact sheet the caption will be written from. Not the caption.

**Write no captions yet.** The words are written at a later stop, where the person chooses them. A sentence written now, to make the script read well, is where a detail gets in that nobody told you: a hall that was "standing empty", a wheel that is "true again". So a beat names its facts and stops there. Where a beat wants something the fact sheet does not hold, such as what the work taught them or what was wrong before they came, put the question in its place.

If the site poses a question, state in planning notes what answers it at the close and which facts support that answer. Its final wording still waits, but its meaning must be decided. A contact link, a job goal or a promise to write an answer later does not supply that meaning.

## Someone with thirty seconds

Say how a visitor who does not scroll gets the name, what the person does, one proof of it, and how to reach them. The first screen carries the first three. A rail jumps to any chapter, and one link skips to the close.

Specify what the opening tells someone outside the field about the person's work, in ordinary words supported by the fact sheet. Explain any unfamiliar title or technical term there. This meaning is part of the plan even while the final captions wait.

## The look and the type

Both come from the world's materials: plates are ceramic on linen, a status page is dark glass, a paper is white with dark ink. Name two or three looks, each as its ground, its ink, one accent, and its surfaces. The person picks by scrolling, once it is built. For the pieces, name two or three of the engine's looks (`stitch/look.ts`); its type is the pieces'.

Two typefaces: one for the text, and one for what is printed in the scenes, which belongs to the object.
