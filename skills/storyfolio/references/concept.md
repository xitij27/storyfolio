# The concept

The second stop: from the fact sheet to three concepts the person can choose between.

## What does not work

- A new coat of paint on the same sections.
- 3D as a backdrop behind the text.
- One 3D scene, however good.
- Another site's idea with the names changed.
- A different effect for every section: a wall of jars, then a door that opens, then a scale that tips. Together they are a tour of effects, and nothing carries the visitor from the first to the last.

## Three things to find

**One question.** The page answers it, and the last screen gives the answer. Look where two sides of the person's work meet, or in the way from one thing to another, or in what all their projects circle. Word it as someone in HR would say it and a colleague would respect. It is about something that interests the person now, and it claims nothing the fact sheet does not hold.

Write it as a question, with a question mark. "Teardown" and "Signal from noise" are themes: they give a page a mood. A question gives the page somewhere to go, and gives the ending a job.

Before choosing the question, state what must be true about the person for it to make sense. Check those premises against the fact sheet's unanswered questions. An employer's field is not the person's contribution. If a premise is unconfirmed, change the question instead of treating it as creative framing.

**One form.** A document or a ritual of the person's own trade that already runs from a beginning to an end, with section names a colleague would know: a paper, a runbook, a job card, a shift handover, a set of drawings. List at least five that the trade has, and take one the person said they work from. Its section names become the chapters, in its order, and no jokes are made in its voice. A chapter holds what its name says.

"Hero, About, Work, Skills, Contact" is the form of a website. It belongs to nobody.

**One object.** The thing that form is physically made of, in five to twelve similar pieces. The pieces carry the person's real work on their faces. They are recognisable standing together at rest. They can be built from simple shapes. They are on screen from the first screen to the last.

The object comes from what the person makes. It is not the trade's emblem: a chef's hat, a server rack, a stethoscope. For anyone who works with data, the emblem is a field of points that gathers into a chart. It fits every analyst alive, which is what is wrong with it.

## What every form has to hold

Map the form's sections onto these roles. Leave out a role that has nothing to hold. Five to eight chapters. Someone with little to show yet gets a shorter story, and not a padded one.

| Role | Holds |
|---|---|
| The opening | Their name, and the question |
| Who | Who is asking |
| Where from | The places and the years |
| How | The way they work |
| What | Some of the work shown working, then all of it |
| What came of it | Outcomes |
| What others say | Their words, one at a time |
| The close | The answer, and how to reach them |

## What the object becomes

In each chapter the same pieces take a new arrangement that shows what the chapter is about: the way they came as a line or a map, how they work as a diagram, one piece of work as an exhibit, every project as a wall whose faces carry real pictures, outcomes as cards. At the close the object is whole again.

Words written on equal pieces are captions moved into the scene. Showing is in the shapes: how many there are, how long, in what order, where they stand. Camille's twenty-four seats are twenty-four plates, the booked ones lit.

**The opening shows where the object comes from.** The answer is the person's own: what they learned from, or what they began with. The object may gather, be put together, unfold, be drawn, develop or switch on.

## Three concepts, each written like this

Make three that differ in kind: in their form or their object, and not in their colour. Write them to `docs/story/concepts.md`.

- **The question.**
- **The form,** with each chapter's name and the role it holds.
- **The object,** and its pieces.
- **What it becomes** in three of the chapters, and the confirmed number, order or proportion one of them draws at true size.
- **The opening and the close.**
- **What it stands on:** the lines of the fact sheet it needs.
- **What it needs from the person:** a picture, someone else's yes.
- **Why it is theirs:** one sentence on why it could not sit on a colleague's site.

Then say which you would choose, and why: the one whose scene shows the most with the captions hidden, not the one that risks least. All three are theirs, so that decides nothing.

For a requested build, open `engine/README.md` and implement all three concepts as local sketches, each with opening, middle and ending. In each middle the pieces draw a confirmed fact or carry the work, readable at the size shown. Inspect each at desktop and phone sizes. Give their working addresses with your recommendation. Planning-only concepts stay in words.

## Test each one before you show it

Write each concept's answer to every test. Rework or drop one that fails.

| Test | Ask |
|---|---|
| Swap | Could it sit unchanged on a colleague's site? Then it is not theirs yet. |
| Backdrop | With the captions hidden, does each chapter's 3D show what the chapter is about, or only that the subject changed, as when one piece steps forward? |
| Depth | Has it a beginning, a middle and an end that follow from one another? |
| Emblem | Is the object the picture that stands for the job? |
| Honesty | Does any sentence in it say more than the fact sheet does: a ranking, a reason, which came first, how a thing was left, a day, a place? |
| Thirty seconds | Can someone who does not scroll get the name, the work, some proof, and how to reach them? |

## Three cases

**A research paper.** The site this skill came from belongs to someone whose work lies between AI research and software engineering. What turned it was a sentence of theirs: "Think of it like a research question." The form became a paper, with a paper's chapters: Abstract, Introduction, Background, Method, Experiments, Results, Peer review, Conclusion. The object became the paper's nine pages, which fold into a globe for where they have lived, stand as the layers of a network for how they work, and hang as a wall of projects.

**A tasting menu.** Camille is a pastry chef, made up for this page. She trained in Lyon and now runs a dessert-only evening once a month: 24 seats, five courses, all sweet. Her trade's forms: the recipe card, the prep list, the tasting menu, the order sheet, the costing sheet. She serves in courses and plans by the menu, so the form is a tasting menu, and each course is a chapter. Each course arrives on a plate, so the object is a stack of plates. The question: "What makes someone remember a dessert?" Turned down first: a whisk and a chef's hat turning in the air, which is the emblem, and one croissant in 3D, which is one scene.

**An incident review.** Jun is a reliability engineer, also made up. Seven years on call. He wrote his team's incident review template. His trade's forms: the runbook, the incident review, the on-call handover, the status page, the dashboard. His work is what happens after something breaks, so the form is an incident review: summary, timeline, root cause, action items. A status page is made of tiles, one to a service, so the object is those tiles. The question: "What keeps a system up at 3 a.m.?" Turned down first: a server rack turning, which is the emblem, and a globe of pings behind the text, which is a backdrop.

**An example shows how to reason. Its concept is not taken.** Camille's menu was reasoned from how Camille serves. A chef who sells one loaf a day from a hatch is not a tasting menu. If you find one of these three concepts in your hand for someone else, it has already failed the swap test: it was made for a person who is not in front of you.
